# The Non-Technical Curriculum: Philosophy, Business, Law, Psychology & How the World Actually Works
*Companion to `curriculum-scratch-to-expert.md` and `ai-robotics-expertise-roadmap.md`. Those documents build technical depth. This one builds judgment — the ability to read a room, a term sheet, a person, a market, or an argument correctly. A technical genius with no judgment gets outmaneuvered by their own cofounder, their own investor, or their own government; this is the curriculum that prevents that.*

## How to use this document

Unlike the technical curriculum, there's no strict prerequisite chain here — philosophy doesn't require economics first. But there is a sensible order of *leverage*: the sections near the top (philosophy, psychology) change how you read everything below them, so front-load them somewhat. Read this the same way as the Inventor's Library in the technical curriculum: continuously, in parallel, for the rest of your life, one book every week or two, never "finished."

**Reuse your existing infrastructure.** Use the same Obsidian vault and commonplace-book habit from the technical setup — add a `09-Worldly-Wisdom` folder alongside `02-Math` through `07-Projects`, and use the same Concept Note template for a book's core argument, the same Feynman-it habit ("explain Kahneman's System 1/2 distinction to a 12-year-old"), and the same weekly public-writing habit. One commonplace book, not two — the best connections happen exactly at the seam between "physics-informed world models" and "how VCs actually think about risk," and you'll only see that seam if both live in the same searchable place.

**The one habit specific to this domain: track belief updates, not just facts.** Technical knowledge is mostly cumulative — you rarely "unlearn" linear algebra. This domain is different: half the value is *changing your mind* about how negotiation, markets, or people actually work. After every book here, write one sentence in your commonplace book: "before this book I believed X, now I believe Y, because Z." If a book didn't move a single belief, you either already knew it cold or weren't reading actively enough.

**Steelman practice.** After any philosophy, politics, or economics book, write the strongest possible argument *against* its thesis before moving on — in your own words, not a strawman. If you can't do this, you don't understand the book's position well enough to actually hold it.

## Philosophy — foundations for how you think, not just what you know

| Book | Author | Why it's here |
|---|---|---|
| *Meditations* | Marcus Aurelius | The single most practically useful philosophy book for anyone under pressure — a Roman emperor's private notes on staying rational and unattached to outcomes. Read this first. |
| *Letters from a Stoic* / *On the Shortness of Life* | Seneca | Stoicism's most quotable, most immediately applicable texts — read alongside Marcus Aurelius. |
| *Enchiridion* | Epictetus | The clearest, shortest statement of the core Stoic distinction (what's in your control vs. not) — genuinely useful for founders, negotiators, and anyone managing risk. |
| *Nicomachean Ethics* | Aristotle | The foundational text on virtue, character, and the good life as a practice, not a feeling — read slowly, this one rewards rereading over years. |
| *The Republic* | Plato | The foundational text of Western political philosophy — read it for the arguments about justice, not just the "cave" metaphor everyone quotes out of context. |
| *Meditations on First Philosophy* | Descartes | The origin of "what can I actually be certain of" — short, and it recalibrates how skeptically you should hold your own beliefs. |
| *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* | David Hume | The classic argument that most of what you call "reasoning" about cause and effect is actually habit — a genuinely destabilizing, useful read. |
| *On Liberty* / *Utilitarianism* | John Stuart Mill | The clearest articulation of harm-principle liberalism and consequentialist ethics — the intellectual scaffolding under most modern Western political argument, whether people know it or not. |
| *Leviathan* | Thomas Hobbes | Why we have states and laws at all — read this before you read anything about tax or regulation, so you understand what they're actually solving for. |
| *Second Treatise of Government* | John Locke | The property-rights and consent-of-the-governed argument that underlies most modern startup/ownership intuitions, whether the reader realizes it or not. |
| *A Theory of Justice* | John Rawls | The "veil of ignorance" — the modern liberal case for fairness as a design principle, extremely relevant to any argument about taxation or redistribution. |
| *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* | Robert Nozick | The libertarian counter-argument to Rawls, same era, same intellectual weight — read both, don't just pick the one you already agree with. |
| *Beyond Good and Evil* | Friedrich Nietzsche | The most serious challenge to conventional morality-as-given — read this for the *method* of questioning inherited values, not to become a Nietzschean. |
| *Tao Te Ching* | Laozi | The classic statement of "the best action is often non-action" — a genuinely useful counterweight to Western hustle-culture instincts. |
| *Antifragile*, *Fooled by Randomness*, *Skin in the Game* | Nassim Nicholas Taleb | Modern applied philosophy of risk, uncertainty, and incentive alignment — the most directly useful philosophy for anyone doing business, investing, or building anything under genuine uncertainty. |
| *Man's Search for Meaning* | Viktor Frankl | Straddles philosophy and psychology — what actually sustains a person under the worst conditions imaginable, from a psychiatrist who survived the Holocaust. Short, and essential. |

**Must-reads if you only have time for four:** *Meditations*, *Nicomachean Ethics*, *Man's Search for Meaning*, *Antifragile*.

## Psychology & Human Nature — how people (including you) actually decide and behave

| Book | Author | Why it's here |
|---|---|---|
| *Thinking, Fast and Slow* | Daniel Kahneman | The single most important book on how human judgment actually works (System 1 intuition vs. System 2 deliberation) — read this before any book on negotiation, business, or investing, because it explains why smart people still get fooled. |
| *Influence* | Robert Cialdini | The six levers of persuasion (reciprocity, commitment, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity) — read it to recognize these being used on you as much as to use them yourself. |
| *Predictably Irrational* | Dan Ariely | Behavioral economics made concrete and experimental — a good practical companion to Kahneman. |
| *Nudge* | Richard Thaler & Cass Sunstein | How "choice architecture" shapes decisions — directly relevant to product design, policy, and understanding why defaults matter so much. |
| *The Righteous Mind* | Jonathan Haidt | Why people who disagree with you politically aren't stupid — a moral-psychology account of why reasoning is mostly post-hoc justification for intuitions you already had. |
| *Behave* | Robert Sapolsky | The biological (neuroscience, endocrinology, evolutionary) basis of human behavior — dense but the deepest "why do people actually act this way" book on this list. |
| *Attached* | Amir Levine & Rachel Heller | Attachment theory applied to adult relationships — genuinely useful for understanding your own and others' behavior under stress, including in cofounder and work relationships. |
| *The Laws of Human Nature* | Robert Greene | Read critically (Greene's framing is manipulative-adjacent and should be read for pattern-recognition, not as an ethics guide) — useful specifically for recognizing the darker patterns in others and yourself. |

**Must-reads:** *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, *Influence*, *The Righteous Mind*.

## Economics — how resources, markets, and incentives actually work

| Resource | Author | Why it's here |
|---|---|---|
| *Basic Economics* | Thomas Sowell | The best zero-jargon entry point — genuinely teaches supply/demand, price signals, and trade-offs without requiring any math. |
| *The Wealth of Nations* (at least Book I) | Adam Smith | Read some of the actual primary source, not just a summary — the division-of-labor argument and "invisible hand" are usually misquoted by people who've never read them. |
| *Free to Choose* | Milton & Rose Friedman | The clearest popular case for market allocation over central planning — read alongside something arguing the other side (see Rawls above, or Acemoglu/Robinson below) to calibrate. |
| *The Road to Serfdom* | Friedrich Hayek | The classic argument about the knowledge problem in central planning — foundational to a huge amount of modern economic and political argument. |
| *Why Nations Fail* | Daron Acemoglu & James A. Robinson | Why some countries get rich and others don't — institutions-over-geography/culture, directly relevant to any question about "why does X country's tech/startup ecosystem work/not work." |
| "How the Economic Machine Works" (30-min video, free) | Ray Dalio | The best 30 minutes you can spend to actually understand credit cycles, debt, and why recessions happen — watch this before reading *Principles*. |
| *Principles* | Ray Dalio | Macro-economic cycles and decision-making frameworks from a working practitioner, not an academic — particularly the *Principles for Dealing with the Changing World Order* material if you want the geopolitics angle. |

**Must-reads:** *Basic Economics*, Dalio's economic-machine video, *Why Nations Fail*.

## Business & Strategy — patterns from people who actually built things

| Book | Author | Why it's here |
|---|---|---|
| *Good to Great* | Jim Collins | The most-cited empirical study of what separates durable great companies from mediocre ones — read critically (some of its case studies aged badly), but the frameworks (Hedgehog Concept, Level 5 Leadership) are genuinely useful. |
| *Competitive Strategy* | Michael Porter | The foundational text on strategic positioning (Five Forces) — dense and academic, but this is where most modern "strategy" vocabulary actually comes from. |
| *The Innovator's Dilemma* | Clayton Christensen | Already in the technical curriculum's Inventor's Library — worth a second read here specifically for the business-strategy angle: why market leaders rationally ignore the innovation that kills them. |
| *Zero to One* | Peter Thiel | Also cross-listed in the technical curriculum — the "secrets" and "monopoly vs. competition" framing is core startup-strategy vocabulary now. |
| *The Hard Thing About Hard Things* | Ben Horowitz | The best available account of what running a company actually feels like when things are going badly — an antidote to survivorship-bias business books. |
| *Shoe Dog* | Phil Knight | Nike's founding story, unusually honest about how close to failure it came — read for the texture of what building something for a decade actually feels like. |
| *The Everything Store* | Brad Stone | Amazon's history — long-term thinking, customer obsession, and Bezos's actual decision-making patterns, from an outside reporter, not a hagiography. |
| *Titan* | Ron Chernow | Rockefeller and Standard Oil — the original playbook for vertical integration and ruthless efficiency, plus the philanthropic second act; useful for pattern-matching against modern tech monopolies. |
| *Bad Blood* | John Carreyrou | Theranos — the essential cautionary tale on what happens when "fake it till you make it" culture meets something (blood diagnostics) where faking it kills people. Read this specifically to calibrate your own ethical lines before you're ever in a position to cross them under pressure. |

**Must-reads:** *The Hard Thing About Hard Things*, *Zero to One*, *Bad Blood*.

## Startups & VC — the specific mechanics of how this industry actually works

| Resource | Author | Why it's here |
|---|---|---|
| *Venture Deals* | Brad Feld & Jason Mendelson | **The** standard reference — goes line by line through a real term sheet and explains what every clause actually means (liquidation preference, anti-dilution, pro-rata rights). If you only read one book in this section, read this one. |
| *The Mom Test* | Rob Fitzpatrick | How to talk to potential customers without accidentally lying to yourself about whether they actually want your product — short, extremely practical, prevents a specific and extremely common founder mistake. |
| *The Lean Startup* | Eric Ries | Build-measure-learn, MVP thinking, validated learning — the vocabulary most of the startup world still uses, whether or not they've read the book. |
| *Mastering the VC Game* | Jeff Bussgang | The VC side of the table, from someone who's been both operator and investor — useful for understanding what you're actually being evaluated on when you pitch. |
| *Angel* | Jason Calacanis | The mechanics of early-stage angel investing specifically — useful even if you're founding, not investing, because it shows you how an investor actually thinks about your deal. |
| **Paul Graham's essays** (paulgraham.com, 200+ essays, free) | Paul Graham | Arguably the single highest-value-per-hour resource in this entire document. Start with "How to Start a Startup," "Do Things That Don't Scale," and "Startup = Growth." Read a new one every week, indefinitely. |
| **Y Combinator Startup School** (free, on-demand, ycombinator.com) | Y Combinator | Free video curriculum covering MVP planning, talking to users, fundraising — direct, current, practitioner-taught. |

**Must-reads:** *Venture Deals*, *The Mom Test*, Paul Graham's essays (ongoing, not a one-time read).

## Law — working literacy for a founder/operator, not a law degree

The goal here isn't to replace a lawyer — it's to never be the person in the room who doesn't understand what they're signing.

- **Contracts, in general**: read one real term sheet (from Venture Deals above), one real NDA, and one real employment offer letter/equity grant, line by line, looking up every term you don't recognize. This single exercise teaches more real contract literacy than any textbook.
- **Intellectual property basics**: understand the difference between a patent, a trademark, and a copyright, and specifically what each actually protects and for how long — this prevents both over-paying for IP protection you don't need and under-protecting something that matters.
- **Corporate structure basics**: understand the practical difference between an LLC, a C-corp, and an S-corp, and *why* venture-backed startups are almost always Delaware C-corps (hint: it's about how preferred stock and future funding rounds work, covered in Venture Deals above).
- **Civic/constitutional literacy**: read a few of *The Federalist Papers* (Nos. 10 and 51 are the standard entry points) — not for founder-specific reasons, but because "how the world works" includes understanding the structure of the government you operate under.
- **The Law** (short essay/book) — Frédéric Bastiat: a philosophical argument about what law is *for*, useful as a counterweight to purely instrumental thinking about regulation.

## Tax & Personal Finance — the layer that quietly determines how much of your success you actually keep

| Resource | Author | Why it's here |
|---|---|---|
| *The Psychology of Money* | Morgan Housel | The best book on the *behavioral* side of personal finance — why smart people make dumb money decisions, and how to not be one of them. |
| *A Random Walk Down Wall Street* | Burton Malkiel | The classic case for low-cost index investing over stock-picking — read this before anyone tries to sell you an actively managed fund. |
| Founder-specific tax literacy: **QSBS (Qualified Small Business Stock)**, **83(b) elections**, capital gains vs. ordinary income | — | These aren't book topics so much as specific mechanisms you must understand before you ever hold equity in a startup — a wrong or missed 83(b) election, in particular, is one of the most common and most expensive mistakes founders make. Learn these from your own accountant/lawyer once you're actually in a position to need them, but know they exist *before* you need them. |
| *Tax-Free Wealth* | Tom Wheelwright | A practitioner's view of how the tax code is actually a set of incentives (for investment, business-building, real estate) rather than just a bill to minimize — read critically, some strategies are aggressive. |
| **Do your own taxes once, by hand or step-by-step in software, understanding every line** | — | The single highest-value practical exercise in this section — you cannot actually evaluate an accountant's advice, or a tax strategy someone pitches you, if you've never once seen how the calculation actually works. |

## Negotiation — a practiced skill, not just a reading topic

| Book | Author | Why it's here |
|---|---|---|
| *Getting to Yes* | Roger Fisher, William Ury, Bruce Patton | The foundational framework (principled negotiation, separate people from the problem, focus on interests not positions, BATNA) — read this for the structure. |
| *Never Split the Difference* | Chris Voss | An FBI hostage negotiator's tactical playbook (tactical empathy, mirroring, calibrated questions, the "that's right" moment) — read this for the psychology and the tactics. Recommended reading order: Voss first for the tactical feel, then Fisher/Ury for the structural framework underneath it. |
| *Bargaining for Advantage* | G. Richard Shell | A more academic treatment that includes negotiation *styles* (people negotiate differently based on personality) — useful for recognizing your own default style and its blind spots. |

**Practice, not just reading:** negotiate something real — a salary, a freelance rate, a purchase, a contract clause — deliberately using a named technique from one of these books (e.g., a calibrated "how" question from Voss, or an explicit BATNA calculation from Fisher/Ury), and write up what happened afterward: what worked, what didn't, what you'd do differently. This is the "prove it" milestone for this entire section — you do not know negotiation from having read about it.

## History & "How the World Works" — the big-picture frame everything else sits inside

| Book | Author | Why it's here |
|---|---|---|
| *Sapiens* | Yuval Noah Harari | A sweeping, opinionated big-picture framing of human history (read critically — some historians dispute specific claims, but the framing device of "shared fictions" as the basis of large-scale cooperation is genuinely useful). |
| *Guns, Germs, and Steel* | Jared Diamond | Why some civilizations industrialized first — a geography/institutions argument to read alongside (and against) Acemoglu/Robinson's institutions-only argument above. |
| *The Art of War* | Sun Tzu | The original strategy classic — short, and its lessons on positioning, deception, and knowing yourself/your opponent map directly onto business and negotiation. |
| *The Prince* | Niccolò Machiavelli | The classic, unsentimental account of how power actually operates versus how we wish it operated — read this specifically to stop being naive about institutional and organizational politics. |
| *The Federalist Papers* (Nos. 10, 51, and a few others) | Hamilton, Madison, Jay | How a specific, successful system of government was actually designed and argued for — civic literacy that most people never actually read past high school, if then. |

## Suggested sequencing (loose — this domain doesn't chain like the technical one)

**First 3-4 months:** the four philosophy/psychology must-reads (Meditations, Nicomachean Ethics, Thinking Fast and Slow, Man's Search for Meaning) — these recalibrate how you read everything else.

**Next 3-4 months:** economics foundations (Basic Economics, Dalio's video, Why Nations Fail) plus negotiation (Voss then Fisher/Ury), since negotiation is the skill you can start *practicing* in parallel with reading immediately.

**Ongoing, interleaved forever:** business/strategy and startup/VC canon as it becomes practically relevant to whatever you're actually building; law and tax literacy specifically at the moments you need them (a term sheet, an equity grant, a filing deadline) rather than pre-emptively memorized; history and communication books interleaved as lighter reading throughout.

## Practice checklist (the "prove it" equivalents for this domain)

- [ ] Negotiate something real using a named technique, and write up the debrief.
- [ ] Read and annotate one real term sheet and one real NDA line by line.
- [ ] File your own taxes once, fully, understanding every line before signing.
- [ ] Write a one-page steelman of a position you disagree with, once a month.
- [ ] Apply at least three ideas from this list (e.g., Mom-Test-style customer conversations, Porter-style positioning, a Voss-style negotiation) to something real you're actually building — a side project, a freelance deal, a club, anything with real stakes, however small.

## How this connects to the technical curriculum

This document is not a detour from `curriculum-scratch-to-expert.md` — it's what makes the technical work *usable*. A founder-researcher who has built the physically-grounded world model from the roadmap document but doesn't understand a term sheet will get diluted out of their own company; one who can't negotiate will get out-negotiated by their first investor; one with no philosophical grounding will burn out chasing the wrong definition of success. Keep both curricula running in the same commonplace book, in parallel, for the same ten years.
