# From High School Algebra to IPhO Gold: A 12-Month Physics Olympiad Curriculum

*A companion document to `curriculum-scratch-to-expert.md`, built the same way: month-by-month, concept-and-problem-driven, every resource named so the plan is independently checkable and reproducible from this file alone.*

## Read this before anything else: an honest calibration

Say this plainly, because a plan that pretends otherwise is a bad plan: **going from high school algebra to International Physics Olympiad (IPhO) gold-medal-level mastery in 12 months is at the extreme edge of what is possible, and for most people it isn't possible at all.** Three things are true simultaneously:

1. **The content itself can be covered in 12 months** by someone with strong general intelligence, no major competing time commitments, and the discipline to run 6-9 focused hours a day, 6-7 days a week, for a full year. This document is built to make that content-coverage goal as rigorous as it can be — no shortcuts, no "conceptual physics" watered-down texts, the same primary sources actual IPhO medalists use.
2. **IPhO gold is not purely a function of content coverage.** Actual gold medalists are almost always people who have already been doing serious physics and competition mathematics for 3-6+ years before their medal year, who have sat multiple national olympiads, and who have the specific, trained-over-years skill of solving an unfamiliar, multi-step problem correctly under a 5-hour time limit with no calculator dependence. Twelve months of even perfect content mastery compresses the *knowledge* gap but only partially compresses the *problem-solving reflexes* gap — that's why this plan puts a full third of its hours into timed problem-solving and mock exams starting in month 3, not month 11.
3. **IPhO participation itself is gated by a national selection process you don't control.** Every country sends a small team (typically 5 students) chosen through its own national olympiad cycle, which runs on its own calendar (often a full academic year, sometimes with cutoffs that don't align with when you started this plan). Reaching "gold-medal-level mastery" and *actually competing at IPhO* are two different finish lines; this document optimizes the first, because it's the one a curriculum can actually control. If you're doing this seriously, find out your national selection process's timeline in parallel with month 1 and work backward from its actual deadlines — that detail cannot come from a generic document like this one.

None of that is a reason not to run the plan — it's the reason to run it exactly as written, without skipping the problem-solving and experimental tracks to "save time" for more reading. Content without trained problem-solving speed does not produce a gold medal; this plan is built to prevent that specific failure mode.

**Assumed starting point:** solid high school algebra (linear/quadratic equations, functions, exponents/logarithms basics, basic trigonometry) and nothing else — no calculus, no calculus-based physics.

**Assumed commitment:** 6-9 hours/day, 6-7 days/week, for 12 months. A suggested weekly rhythm and daily template are given below. If your actual available time is lower, stretch the calendar proportionally rather than cutting the problem sets or the experimental track — those are the two components a rushed self-study plan drops first, and they're the two that actually separate a medalist from someone who "knows the material."

## The two (then three) tracks

Olympiad physics is not "learn the theory, then do problems" in sequence — the theory and the problems train different things and have to run in parallel from day one, exactly the way the Fast Track in `curriculum-scratch-to-expert.md` insists math and projects run together.

- **Track A — Theory & derivation.** Work through a primary textbook chapter by chapter, deriving every result yourself on paper before checking the book's derivation, not just reading it. If you can't reproduce a derivation cold two days later, it isn't learned yet — this is where a spaced-repetition tool (Anki) earns its keep for formulas and key derivation steps.
- **Track B — Timed problem-solving.** Every single week, without exception, spend real time on hard, unfamiliar problems from the problem/pset books below, under time pressure that gets progressively closer to real exam conditions as the year goes on. This is the track that actually builds olympiad reflexes; Track A alone does not.
- **Track C — Experimental physics** (starts month 2, runs every week after). IPhO's score is split roughly 50/50 between a theoretical exam and a 5-hour experimental exam. A candidate who can derive Maxwell's equations from memory but has never propagated a measurement uncertainty or fit a line to noisy data with a ruler and a stopwatch will lose half the available points. This is the single most commonly under-prepared part of self-study olympiad prep — treat it as a co-equal subject, not an afterthought.

## Daily schedule template (6-9 hour day)

| Block | Duration | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 90-120 min | Track A: new theory, active derivation, note-taking |
| 2 | 90-120 min | Track A continued, or math-track work that theory chapter needs (see month-by-month "math needed") |
| — | 15-30 min | Break |
| 3 | 90-120 min | Track B: problem sets on the current topic, worked with real effort before checking solutions |
| 4 | 60-90 min | Track B continued, or (2x/week) Track C experimental session |
| 5 | 30-45 min | Review: Anki spaced-repetition pass, error log entry for anything you got wrong that week (see below) |

**The error log.** Keep a running document (or an Obsidian vault, per `curriculum-scratch-to-expert.md`'s note-taking stack) of every problem you got wrong, categorized by *why*: conceptual gap, careless algebra, ran out of time, didn't see the trick. Review it weekly. Olympiad coaches consistently report this single habit produces more score improvement per hour than additional content coverage once the basics are down — you are training out your own specific, repeating failure modes, which no textbook can do for you.

**Weekly rhythm:** 6 days on this schedule, 1 lighter day for a full past-paper mock under real time conditions (see the mock-exam calendar) or full rest — do not skip rest entirely for a full year; burnout is a bigger risk to this plan than any single week's content.

## Month-by-month curriculum

### Month 1 — Precalculus fluency, intro calculus, and the physicist's vector toolkit
**Goal:** eliminate any algebra/trig fragility completely (it will silently sabotage every physics derivation for the rest of the year if not fixed now), and get through single-variable differential calculus.
**Math (Track A):** Full review of algebra II and trigonometry (identities, unit circle, graphing) via the OpenStax *Precalculus* free textbook (Jay Abramson et al.) or Michael Sullivan's *Precalculus*; then limits, derivatives, and differentiation rules from James Stewart's *Calculus: Early Transcendentals*, chapters 1-3, paired with MIT OCW 18.01 (Single Variable Calculus) lectures for a second explanation of anything unclear.
**Physics:** vectors (addition, dot/cross product, components) and one-dimensional then two-dimensional kinematics — Kleppner & Kolenkow, *An Introduction to Mechanics*, chapter 1, and David Morin, *Introduction to Classical Mechanics*, chapter 1.
**Problem sets (Track B):** every end-of-chapter problem in K&K ch. 1 and Morin ch. 1; Stewart ch. 1-3 odd-numbered problems for calculus fluency.
**Milestone:** solve 20 mixed algebra/trig/vector problems from a fresh source (e.g., an F=ma practice set, see resource stack) in 90 minutes with zero look-ups.

### Month 2 — Calculus completion, Newton's laws, energy and momentum
**Math:** integration techniques and the fundamental theorem of calculus (Stewart ch. 4-7); begin *Div, Grad, Curl, and All That* (H.M. Schey) informally alongside for physical intuition about gradients, which you'll need very soon.
**Physics:** Newton's three laws, forces (friction, tension, springs), work-energy theorem, conservation of energy, momentum and collisions — K&K ch. 2-4, Morin ch. 2-3.
**Track C (experimental physics — starts this month):** John R. Taylor, *An Introduction to Error Analysis: The Study of Uncertainties in Physical Measurements*, chapters 1-4 (significant figures, propagation of uncertainty, standard deviation, fitting a line to data). Do a real, physical experiment this month (a pendulum timed with a phone stopwatch is enough) and write it up with proper uncertainty propagation.
**Problem sets:** K&K ch. 2-4 and Morin ch. 2-3 in full; begin Irodov, *Problems in General Physics*, mechanics chapter, doing problems as they become accessible.
**Milestone:** a diagnostic mock exam at the F=ma (AAPT) level — see mock-exam calendar below.

### Month 3 — Multivariable/vector calculus, rotational dynamics, and gravitation
**Math:** partial derivatives, multiple integrals, and vector calculus (gradient, divergence, curl, line/surface integrals) — Stewart ch. 12-16, finished alongside a full pass of Schey's *Div, Grad, Curl, and All That*, and MIT OCW 18.02 lectures.
**Physics:** rotational kinematics and dynamics, torque, moment of inertia, angular momentum, rigid-body motion, Kepler's laws and gravitation — K&K ch. 5-9, Morin ch. 8-9 and the gravitation chapter.
**Problem sets:** K&K and Morin rotation/gravitation chapters in full; continue Irodov mechanics; start Jaan Kalda's free *Mechanics* handout (see resource stack) — Kalda's problems are explicitly written at olympiad difficulty and are the single best bridge between textbook mechanics and IPhO-style mechanics problems.
**Milestone:** first exposure to real past-IPhO mechanics problems (see mock-exam calendar) — don't expect to solve them yet, but start the habit of attempting one per week.

### Month 4 — Linear algebra, ODEs, and oscillations/waves
**Math:** linear algebra through eigenvalues/eigenvectors — Gilbert Strang, *Introduction to Linear Algebra* (paired with MIT OCW 18.06 and, for geometric intuition, 3Blue1Brown's *Essence of Linear Algebra* series); ordinary differential equations — Tenenbaum & Pollard, *Ordinary Differential Equations* (Dover), focusing on linear ODEs with constant coefficients, which is exactly what SHM and RLC circuits need.
**Physics:** simple harmonic motion, damped/driven oscillators, resonance, coupled oscillators and normal modes, the wave equation, superposition, standing waves — A.P. French, *Vibrations and Waves*, in full, plus Morin's oscillation chapters.
**Problem sets:** French's problems in full; Kalda's *Mechanics* handout completed; begin Kalda's oscillations material if included in that handout's later sections.
**Milestone:** timed 3-hour mixed mechanics+oscillations problem set under exam conditions (no notes, no calculator beyond arithmetic).

### Month 5 — Math methods, electrostatics, and DC circuits
**Math:** complex numbers and their use in oscillation/AC problems, an introduction to Fourier series — Mary Boas, *Mathematical Methods in the Physical Sciences*, chapters 2 (complex numbers) and 7 (Fourier series).
**Physics:** Coulomb's law, electric field and potential, Gauss's law, conductors, capacitors and dielectrics, DC circuits (Kirchhoff's laws, RC circuits) — Purcell & Morin, *Electricity and Magnetism* (3rd ed.), chapters 1-4.
**Problem sets:** Purcell & Morin ch. 1-4 problems in full (this book's problems are already olympiad-adjacent in difficulty); Irodov's electricity chapters in parallel; begin Kalda's *Electromagnetism* handout.
**Milestone:** build and characterize a real RC circuit (even a breadboard + multimeter setup) and compare the measured time constant to the calculated one, with proper uncertainty analysis (Track C).

### Month 6 — Magnetostatics, induction, AC circuits, and first full mock exam
**Physics:** magnetic fields and forces, Biot-Savart law, Ampère's law, electromagnetic induction, Faraday's and Lenz's laws, inductance, AC circuits and impedance, Maxwell's equations (qualitative unification) — Purcell & Morin ch. 5-8; supplement with Griffiths, *Introduction to Electrodynamics*, for a second derivation style on anything unclear.
**Math:** phasors and complex impedance (an application of month 5's complex numbers); light touch on partial differential equations via Boas ch. 8, enough to appreciate the wave equation's PDE structure.
**Problem sets:** Purcell & Morin ch. 5-8 in full; Kalda's *Electromagnetism* handout completed; Irodov E&M chapters completed.
**Milestone:** **first full-length, timed, USAPhO-difficulty mock exam** covering mechanics + E&M (see mock-exam calendar). Log every miss in the error log and spend the first week of month 7 closing the biggest gaps before moving on.

### Month 7 — Waves, geometric and physical optics
**Physics:** reflection/refraction, lenses and mirrors (thin-lens equation, ray tracing), dispersion, interference (double-slit, thin films), diffraction (single slit, gratings), polarization — Eugene Hecht, *Optics*, selected chapters (geometric optics chapters, then interference/diffraction/polarization chapters; the full physical/quantum optics chapters at the back are lower priority for IPhO and can be skimmed).
**Math:** finish Boas ch. 7-8 (Fourier analysis and PDEs) alongside, since diffraction is most cleanly understood as a Fourier-transform relationship.
**Problem sets:** Hecht's problems on the assigned chapters; Kalda's *Optics/Waves* handout; continue weekly past-IPhO problem attempts, now including optics problems.
**Track C:** an optics experiment (measuring a diffraction grating's line spacing from a laser pointer and a known wavelength, or the focal length of a lens by the object-image method) with full uncertainty write-up.

### Month 8 — Thermodynamics and statistical mechanics
**Physics:** the four laws of thermodynamics, ideal gases, kinetic theory, heat engines and refrigerators, entropy, the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution, an introduction to statistical mechanics (partition functions at a qualitative/light-quantitative level) — Daniel Schroeder, *An Introduction to Thermal Physics*, cover to cover; this is consistently rated the best-written undergraduate text for exactly this purpose and its problems are strong.
**Problem sets:** Schroeder's problems in full; Irodov's thermodynamics chapters; Kalda's *Thermodynamics/Statistical Physics* handout.
**Track C:** a thermodynamics experiment (e.g., measuring the specific heat of a metal by mixing calorimetry, or verifying Boyle's law with a syringe and pressure gauge) with uncertainty analysis.
**Milestone:** second full-length mock exam, mechanics + E&M + thermodynamics combined.

### Month 9 — Special relativity, quantum basics, atomic and nuclear physics
**Physics:** special relativity (time dilation, length contraction, relativistic energy-momentum, spacetime diagrams) — Edwin Taylor & John Archibald Wheeler, *Spacetime Physics*, in full (this book is built entirely around solved problems, which fits Track B directly); modern/atomic/nuclear physics (photoelectric effect, Compton scattering, Bohr model, de Broglie waves, the uncertainty principle, basic atomic structure, radioactive decay, nuclear binding energy) — Kenneth Krane, *Modern Physics*, the relevant chapters.
**Enrichment (optional, time permitting):** Griffiths, *Introduction to Quantum Mechanics*, chapter 1-2 for a deeper, first-principles pass at the Schrödinger equation — IPhO rarely requires solving the Schrödinger equation itself, but understanding where quantization actually comes from pays off on conceptual problems.
**Problem sets:** Taylor & Wheeler in full; Krane's end-of-chapter problems; Kalda does not have a dedicated relativity/quantum handout, so lean on past-IPhO modern-physics problems here directly.
**Milestone:** **first full mock IPhO** — two 5-hour theoretical papers (or one, if time-constrained) drawn from an actual past IPhO problem set, taken cold, under real exam conditions.

### Month 10 — Analytical mechanics, fluid mechanics, and cross-topic synthesis
**Physics:** Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics (this is the tool that makes the hardest IPhO mechanics problems — coupled constrained systems, normal modes of complicated systems — tractable in minutes instead of pages of Newtonian bookkeeping) — John R. Taylor, *Classical Mechanics*, the Lagrangian/Hamiltonian chapters, supplemented by Morin's own Lagrangian chapter; fluid mechanics basics (pressure, Bernoulli's equation, continuity, viscosity qualitatively) — Kalda's *Hydrodynamics* handout is the primary source here, since most standard intro texts barely touch fluids and IPhO does test them.
**Problem sets:** Taylor's Lagrangian-mechanics problems; a full second pass through Kalda's *Mechanics* and *Electromagnetism* handouts' hardest problems, now with the full toolkit available; begin working *200 Puzzling Physics Problems* and *200 More Puzzling Physics Problems* (Gnädig, Honyek, Vigh) systematically, one problem a day minimum — these are explicitly written to require the kind of creative cross-topic insight IPhO rewards, not routine plug-and-chug.
**Track C:** a fluids experiment (e.g., Bernoulli's principle via a simple flow setup, or measuring viscosity with falling-ball terminal velocity).
**Milestone:** second full mock IPhO, plus a dedicated 5-hour mock experimental exam using a past official IPhO experimental problem.

### Month 11 — Intensive olympiad problem-solving sprint
**Focus:** this month is almost entirely Track B and Track C. Work systematically through *every* past IPhO problem from the last 15-20 years, organized by topic, timing yourself against the real per-problem time budget (roughly 100 minutes per full problem on the theory papers). Alongside that: finish *200 (More) Puzzling Physics Problems* if not already done; work deeply into Irodov's remaining unsolved chapters; do a full pass of I.E. Irodov-adjacent Russian-tradition problem books such as S.S. Krotov's *Aptitude Test Problems in Physics*, which are explicitly built for exactly this kind of rapid-fire olympiad drilling.
**Track C:** two more full 5-hour mock experimental exams from past official IPhO experimental problems, including the write-up and data-analysis portion, not just the measurement.
**Milestone:** third and fourth full mock IPhO theory papers, one per week, each followed by a full error-log review before the next.

### Month 12 — Final mock exams, weak-topic remediation, and taper
**Weeks 1-2:** targeted remediation, driven entirely by the error log — re-derive, from the relevant primary textbook, every concept that has caused a repeated miss across the year's mock exams. This is not new content; it's closing specific, identified gaps.
**Week 3:** final full mock IPhO (both theory papers plus a full experimental exam), under conditions as close to real exam-day logistics as you can arrange (a proctor if possible, no phone, real 5-hour blocks).
**Week 4:** a genuine taper — reduced hours, light review only (Anki deck, re-reading your own error log, skimming formula sheets), full sleep, no new material. Peaking for a specific exam date requires backing off before it, exactly like tapering before an athletic competition; cramming through the final week reliably makes exam-day performance worse, not better.
**Milestone:** the plan's terminal "prove it": cold-solve a full, previously-unseen past IPhO paper (theory + experiment) within the official time limits and score it yourself against the official marking scheme, which is published alongside the official problems.

## After month 12

If you've hit the terminal milestone (cold-solving a full past IPhO paper within time and scoring gold-medal-range against the official scheme), the honest next steps are: (1) confirm and enter your country's actual national olympiad selection process if you haven't already — that process, not this document, determines whether you get to compete; (2) keep the weekly problem-solving and mock-exam cadence going, since olympiad reflexes decay without maintenance, the same way competitive-athletic conditioning does; (3) if your national process has multiple selection rounds spread over more than a year, use the extra time for the two things this compressed plan necessarily under-weighted — deeper Lagrangian/Hamiltonian mechanics and a genuinely broad base of solved problems (hundreds more than any 12-month plan can fit) — both of which are exactly what separates a strong participant from a repeat gold medalist.
